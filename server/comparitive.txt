3. Comparative Analysis.
Common Findings.
AI Integration in Driving Decisions.
All studies reviewed have concluded that artificial intelligence is the key factor driving in autonomous vehicles. Irrespective of their field of specialization, all authors acknowledge that AI empowers vehicles to comprehend information, make decisions, and adapt to changing road conditions. The use of AI in conjunction with IoT and cloud computing enables data exchange effectively, as Tyagi and Aswathy (2021) point out. Using reinforcement learning and hybrid AI models, vehicles can learn from experience to improve their ability to respond safely and efficiently to changing environments, as shown by Peng et al. (2021) and Benamar d. 2022 respectively.
Learning-based decision systems, which can self-improve over time, are predicted to be the driving system of the future, as suggested by these studies. The shift from traditional rule-based algorithms to adaptive models that can anticipate and predict outcomes and handle uncertainty is occurring. There has been a radical change in the idea of autonomy, moving from following programmed instructions to evolving through intelligent experience.
Learning-Based Perception.
Most papers agree that perception and the environment are crucial for understanding. As noted by Guan et al. (2021) and Kozlowski p. 2024, the reliability of an autonomous vehicle is dependent on its ability to interpret its surroundings with great precision. With the help of cameras, LiDAR (energy-sharp) and radar sensors, perception systems using deep learning have enabled vehicles to more accurately identify obstacles, road signs or lanes. Shinohara et al. (2024) point out that vision-based imitation learning allows vehicles to mimic human driving strategies through camera inputs, demonstrating that visual comprehension is not just a technical requirement but also enables individuals to drive as if they were humans.
These studies reveal that the foundation of autonomous driving is built on multi-sensor fusion and visual intelligence. The. Neural networks have advanced so that now vehicles are not just 'blind machines' tracking maps but also able to perceive and interpret the environment in real time. Why?

Differences.
Technical vs. Conceptual Orientation.
The paper's research focus stands out as one of the most pronounced differences. The interdisciplinary approach of Tyagi and Aswathy (2021) involves examining the ecosystem of intelligent vehicles through an underlying conceptual framework that includes IoT, AI, and regulation. By way of contrast, Peng et al. (2021) and Shinohara (2010–24) take a technically very specific approach, building and testing end-to-end models through reinforcement and imitation learning respectively. This research lays the theoretical foundation for how intelligent vehicles should operate, while the latter convert those concepts into quantifiable performance outcomes. The study is divided into two parts.

Gaps Across Studies.
The Reality Gap.
One of the enduring research gaps is the inconsistency between simulation and real-world conditions, as evidenced by numerous studies. As noted by Peng et al. (2021) and Shinohara a.g. (224), it is apparent that AI models trained in virtual simulations exhibit high performance when subjected to controlled conditions, but low performance under unpredictable real-world scenarios. Unfolding vulnerabilities are frequently discovered due to weather changes, glare, road markings, and unexpected pedestrian behavior. One of the primary impediments to the widespread deployment of self-driving cars is the "real world gap.".
Lack of Standardization.
A significant gap is the lack of unified testing and evaluation standards. Various datasets, performance metrics and experimental setups are used by each research group.How? Grosse & al, 2025 argue that the absence of standardised criteria makes it hard to compare results or set clear safety thresholds. Measuring genuine progress across the field becomes almost impossible without common testing methods. Nevertheless, this issue highlights the necessity for worldwide cooperation and regulatory mechanisms that outline the assessment of AI-driven infrastructure before its commercial launch.
Ethical and Legal Ambiguities.
Finally, many studies indicate that ethical and legal issues remain unresolved. While AI can improve driving safety, there are still important questions to consider regarding liability and moral responsibility. The manufacturer, AI developer, or vehicle owner may be at fault for an autonomous vehicle if it crashes. This remains uncertain. In 2025, Grosse et al. advocated for the implementation of open accountability mechanisms in all settings, while Tyagi and Aswathy (2021) highlighted the increasing dangers associated with cyber attacks that could potentially harm users.
